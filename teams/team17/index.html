Why Students Drop Out of Online Courses, and How Early We Can Tell

Online learning platforms record clicks, logins and assignment submissions. We use these learning traces to understand student withdrawal and test how early an at-risk student can be identified—while there is still time to help.

Problem statement

In an online course, a student can disengage without an instructor noticing immediately. By the time a missed assessment or final result reveals the problem, the opportunity to help may have passed. Our project investigates whether student background information and learning-platform activity can predict withdrawal, and how early those predictions become useful.

We focus on three questions: which factors are associated with withdrawal; how prediction changes when we add early course activity; and whether simple warning rules can identify students who may need support without relying on a complex machine-learning model.

Dataset

We use the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), a public, anonymised dataset from online courses offered by the UK Open University in 2013–2014. The analysis covers 32,593 enrolments from 28,785 unique students and combines seven linked source tables, including student information, course and assessment records, registration information, and virtual learning environment (VLE) activity. The VLE table contains approximately 10.6 million activity records.

The outcome is whether an enrolment ended as Withdrawn. The original final-result categories—Pass, Distinction, Fail and Withdrawn—are also examined when comparing the full outcome prediction task.

Approach

  • Clean and validate: handle missing-value markers, standardise an inconsistent income-band category, and retain enrolments with no website activity.
  • Engineer features: summarise daily VLE records into student-level measures such as total clicks, active days and weeks, page types visited, inactivity gaps and weekly clicks; add assignment submission and mark summaries.
  • Avoid information leakage: do not use unregistration dates or end-of-course exam marks as predictors of withdrawal.
  • Compare prediction timing: evaluate background-only features, background plus the first two weeks of clicks, and full-course activity.
  • Interpret patterns: inspect withdrawal rates across engagement, course-load and background groups, and use PCA to assess overlap among features.

Methods and tools

For binary withdrawal prediction, we compared Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM) and XGBoost. We also evaluated four-class final-result prediction and used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to study feature redundancy. The train/test split was grouped by student ID so the same student could not appear in both sets.

Tools: Python, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, XGBoost and data visualisation tools.

Why this matters

Withdrawal is more common than failure in this dataset: 31.2% of enrolments withdrew, compared with 21.6% that failed. If early signals can help staff reach students before they disappear from the course, the analysis could support timely, human-centred outreach rather than waiting for final marks.
