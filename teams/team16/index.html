Predicting NYC Taxi Trip Duration through Spatial Network and Regression Analysis

Given a taxi trip's pickup location, dropoff location and pickup time, we predict how long the ride will take — by turning the city itself into a graph and feeding its structure into regression models built from scratch.

Problem statement

Our goal is to predict the duration (in seconds) of a yellow-taxi trip in New York City, given only four coordinates (pickup/dropoff latitude and longitude) and a pickup timestamp. This is the task behind Kaggle's well-known "NYC Taxi Trip Duration" competition, re-framed here as a course project in applied linear algebra, graph theory and regression: can we substantially improve a plain linear model just by (a) engineering better geometric/temporal features, and (b) representing the city's road network as a graph and feeding its spectral structure into the regression?

Dataset

We use the public Kaggle NYC Taxi Trip Duration dataset — about 1.46 million real yellow-taxi rides recorded between January and June 2016, with pickup/dropoff coordinates, pickup timestamp, passenger count, vendor ID, and the trip duration label.

During cleaning we drop trips that are physically impossible (shorter than 60 seconds, longer than 24 hours, outside New York City, or with zero passengers) — only 0.89% of rows are removed. Because trip duration is heavily right-skewed, we transform the target with y′ = log(1 + y), which turns it into a roughly bell-shaped distribution that a linear model can fit well.

Approach

The project is organised into five stages:

  • Clean the data — remove impossible trips and log-transform the skewed target.
  • Engineer 16 features — 5 geometric (Haversine distance, bearing, Manhattan distance, north–south / east–west components), 6 temporal (hour, day of week, month, weekend/rush-hour flags, with hour and day cyclically encoded on the unit circle), and 2 interaction terms (distance × rush hour, distance × weekend).
  • Build a spatial network — cluster NYC into 50 zones with K-means, build a 50×50 pickup–dropoff adjacency matrix, derive the symmetric graph Laplacian Lsym = I − D−1/2AD−1/2 (proved positive semi-definite), and use its eigenvectors for spectral clustering (5 natural neighbourhoods) and four centrality scores (degree, betweenness, PageRank, eigenvector) per zone — 13 new graph-based features in total.
  • Fit three models from scratch — Ordinary Least Squares via the normal equation, Gradient Descent with a provably safe step size η < 2/λmax(H), and Ridge Regression with α chosen by 5-fold cross-validation.
  • Validate statistically — Welch's t-test to compare models, a 200-iteration bootstrap for coefficient confidence intervals, condition-number analysis of XTX for numerical stability, and an ablation study across four nested feature sets.

Tools used

Python, pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, NetworkX, Matplotlib, Seaborn, JupyterLab.
