Can a Machine Truly Understand Movie Reviews?

We trained and compared ten machine learning models on 100,000 real IMDB reviews to classify sentiment, predict 1–10 star ratings, and discover natural review clusters.

Problem Statement

When people write movie reviews online, they don't just say “this is good” or “this is bad”. They use sarcasm, tell stories, praise the cinematography while criticizing the plot, and express strong emotions through punctuation and formatting.

Our Core Question

Can a computer automatically read a review written in everyday English and accurately understand how much the viewer liked or disliked the film?

To answer this question thoroughly, we formulated three learning tasks:

  • Task 1 • Binary Classification: Predict whether a review is categorically Positive or Negative. We tested models ranging from simple word counts up to fine-tuned BERT transformers.
  • Task 2 • Continuous Regression: Go beyond binary labels to predict the reviewer's exact numerical score on a continuous scale of 1 to 10 stars based purely on their text.
  • Task 3 • Unsupervised Clustering: Remove all ratings and sentiment labels completely, testing whether clustering algorithms can discover natural groupings on raw text on their own.

Why is this hard for a machine?

  • Negation: “This movie was not bad at all.” A simple model sees the word “bad” and predicts negative, even though the viewer liked it.
  • Sarcasm: “A masterpiece of terrible acting.” The praise word “masterpiece” confuses models that don't understand context.
  • Mixed Opinions: “Great visuals, but a boring and slow story.” Models must weigh contrasting sentiments across long paragraphs.

Dataset

We used the benchmark IMDB Large Movie Review Dataset, containing 100,000 real reviews written by real moviegoers. We systematically structured the data into three subsets of 50,000 reviews each:

Subset Name Size Description & Use Case
1. Classification Set 50,000 reviews 25,000 training and 25,000 testing reviews, perfectly balanced 50/50 (positive vs. negative) to prevent class imbalance biases.
2. Rating Prediction Set 50,000 reviews 50,000 reviews where each file has the reviewer's exact 1–10 star rating extracted directly from the raw filename metadata (e.g., 1234_7.txt = 7/10).
3. Unsupervised Set 50,000 reviews 50,000 completely unlabelled reviews with all scores and folder tags stripped away, serving as a pure sandbox for K-Means and GMM clustering.

Data Preprocessing

Raw text scraped from the web is messy. Before feeding any text into our models, we put all 100,000 reviews through a 4-step cleaning pipeline:

  • HTML Tag Removal: Over 58% of the raw reviews contained <br /> break tags from website markup. We stripped all HTML tags using regular expressions so models learn language instead of web code.
  • Symbol Decoding: Web entities like &amp; and &quot; were unescaped back into clean characters.
  • Whitespace Normalization: Multiple consecutive spaces, rogue tabs, and random line breaks were collapsed into clean paragraphs.
  • Deduplication: We identified and removed 96 duplicate reviews in the training set and 199 in the test set, preventing data leakage between splits.

Turning Words into Numbers

Computers do math on numbers, not words. We tested two main feature extraction approaches:

  • TF-IDF with N-Grams: For traditional models (Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, XGBoost), we used Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency. By including 2-word bigrams like “worst movie” or “highly recommend”, we preserved local word-order information.
  • Token Embeddings & Self-Attention: For deep learning (BiLSTM and BERT), text was split into subword tokens and mapped to dense vector embeddings. BERT uses bidirectional self-attention to read every word relative to the whole sentence simultaneously.

Key Dataset Discoveries (6 Documented Anomalies)

While exploring the dataset, we identified six fascinating behavioral and linguistic patterns in how people write movie reviews:

  • 1. The Missing Middle: Ratings 5 and 6 are almost completely absent from the dataset. First, people rarely write online reviews unless they loved a film (10/10) or hated it (1/10). Second, the dataset creators removed neutral ratings to ensure clean binary polarity.
  • 2. The “Anger Index” in Punctuation: 1-star negative reviews contain nearly 4 times more exclamation marks (!!!) and ALL-CAPS words than 10-star reviews. Unhappy viewers express anger through punctuation, while positive reviews rely on descriptive adjectives.
  • 3. Strong Opinions Make People Write More: Reviews with 1 star or 10 stars average over 1,420 characters, compared to under 950 characters for moderate reviews. Polarized opinions motivate users to write miniature essays.
  • 4. Single Words Give Zero Signal; Word Pairs Matter: Single words like “movie” or “film” appear everywhere and give zero clue about sentiment. But 2-word pairs (bigrams) like “waste time” or “must see” immediately dictate the rating.
  • 5. Long Reviews Challenge Standard LSTMs: Standard recurrent neural networks read word-by-word. When reviews are 200+ words long, LSTMs struggle to remember the beginning of the text, giving Logistic Regression a slight edge. BERT overcomes this by processing the entire sequence at once.
  • 6. Mixed Reviews Need Soft Probabilities: Reviews praising acting while slamming the plot cannot fit neatly into a single hard cluster. Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) handle this much better by assigning continuous percentages (e.g., 60% positive, 40% negative).
